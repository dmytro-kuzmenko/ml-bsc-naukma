Polynomial regression and overfitting

A polynomial is fitted by least squares to noisy one-dimensional data drawn from y = sin(2πx) + noise. The first slider sets the complexity of the model and the second sets how much training data is available. Seventy percent of the points are used for training and the remaining thirty are held back for testing, so you can watch underfitting turn into overfitting as the degree rises.

Things worth trying

  1. Start at degree 3 with 80 points. Both errors sit close to the noise floor, which is what a good fit looks like.
  2. Push the degree up to 12, and then to 15. The curve starts to chase individual points, and the gap between the two errors widens. That gap is the signature of overfitting, which is to say of high variance.
  3. Drop the degree to 1 or 2. Both errors rise sharply, because a straight line simply cannot bend into the shape of the data. That is underfitting, which is to say high bias.
  4. Raise the number of points to 200 and repeat the second experiment. The same high degree now overfits far less. More data moves the best complexity upwards.
  5. Reduce the number of points to 20. Even a degree of 10 now produces large errors on the test data. Notice the message that appears when the degree has to be reduced because there are too few points to fit the polynomial you asked for.
