Module 3 · Learning a Boundary (Classification)

Nearest neighbors: the decision boundary and the number of neighbors

A nearest-neighbor classifier labels each point by taking a majority vote among the k training points closest to it. A small number of neighbors produces a jagged boundary that has effectively memorized the training set; a large number produces a smooth and much simpler one. The colored background shows the predicted class at every point of a grid of 90 by 90 positions, and a training point that its own neighbors get wrong is marked with a cross.

Things worth trying

  1. Set the number of neighbors to 1. The boundary is jagged and accuracy on the training data is 100 percent, because every point is its own nearest neighbor. The model has memorized the data. The leave-one-out figure is the honest estimate here.
  2. Raise the number slowly to 5, then 15, then 31. The boundary smooths out, and the leave-one-out accuracy first rises and then begins to fall again as the model becomes too simple to follow the real shape.
  3. Compare 1 neighbor with 31. Which of the two boundaries looks more reasonable for this data, and which one would you trust on points you have not seen?
  4. Push the number to 101, which is larger than the dataset itself. The boundary collapses to a single class and the leave-one-out accuracy collapses with it, because a neighborhood that large is no longer local to anything.
  5. Compare the crosses at 1 neighbor and at 15. With a single neighbor no training point can possibly be wrong; with a moderate number, a few points near the boundary are, and that is usually the better sign.
