Module 7 · Finding Structure Without Labels

k-means clustering and principal component analysis

Two ways of finding structure in data that carries no labels, both shown on fixed data generated from a fixed seed. In the first panel you advance the k-means algorithm one iteration at a time and watch its inertia fall. In the second you see the two principal directions of a correlated cloud of points, and you can flatten the cloud onto the first of them.

Ninety points were drawn from three separate clusters, using a fixed seed. The slider sets the number of clusters the algorithm looks for, and changing it restarts the algorithm from the same deterministic starting centers. One step performs a single iteration: every point is assigned to its nearest center, and then every center moves to the mean of the points assigned to it. Running to the end repeats that until nothing changes.

Take a few steps and watch the centers settle into the three groups. Inertia falls whenever another cluster is added, so it can never tell you on its own that a number is too large. What it can show is where the fall stops being dramatic, and here the largest relative drop is at three clusters. That bend is what people mean by the elbow.

A cloud of 120 points in two dimensions, generated from a fixed seed, is stretched along one direction. Principal component analysis finds the direction along which the data varies most, and the direction at right angles to it, which carries whatever variation is left. The slider moves every point gradually from where it actually lies to where it would land if it were flattened onto the first of those directions.

Drag the slider all the way to 1. Every point collapses onto a single line, and a cloud that lived in two dimensions is now described by one number each, keeping the larger part of the variation.

Things worth trying

  1. Clustering. With three clusters, take a few single steps. The centers move from their starting positions into the middle of the three groups, and the inertia falls quickly as they do.
  2. Clustering. Run to the end with two, three and four clusters, and note the final inertia in each case. The largest relative drop is at three, which is the reasoning behind choosing a number of clusters from the bend in the curve.
  3. Clustering. Try one cluster, which puts a single center at the mean of everything, and then six, which splits each real group in two. The inertia keeps falling, which is exactly why it can never tell you on its own that six is the better answer.
  4. Principal components. Flatten the data completely. The elongated structure survives, because the first component alone accounts for about 94 percent of the variance.
  5. Principal components. Hide the second component and consider what the flattening throws away: the small amount of scatter across the narrow direction, which is the remaining 6 percent or so of the variance.
